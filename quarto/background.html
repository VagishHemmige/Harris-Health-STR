Clinical background

Author

Vagish Hemmige

This page covers the clinical side of the study: why staying in care and taking ART matter, what single-tablet regimens are, the setting, the regimens compared, and what was found. The general methods are on Methodological background, and what this study chose on Study design and methods; a suggested reading order is at the end of this page.

Why staying in care and taking ART matter

Antiretroviral therapy (ART) does not cure HIV, but taken every day it keeps the virus suppressed, lets the immune system recover, and stops sexual transmission: in the HPTN 052 trial, starting ART early reduced linked transmission to partners by 96% (Cohen et al. 2011). The benefit depends on a chain of steps, often drawn as the HIV care continuum: diagnosis, linkage to care, staying in care, receiving ART, and viral suppression. Each step loses people. In the United States around 2010, only about one in five people living with HIV was estimated to have a suppressed viral load (Gardner et al. 2011).

Two steps of that chain are this study’s outcomes:

  • Staying in care. Patients with gaps in HIV care in their first year die sooner. Among veterans starting ART, those with visits in fewer quarters of the first year had higher mortality over the next years (Giordano et al. 2007), and in a Birmingham clinic, patients who missed a visit in their first year had more than twice the mortality of those who did not (Mugavero et al. 2009). How retention is measured is on Methodological background.
  • Taking ART. Early studies of protease inhibitors found that viral suppression needed very high adherence: virologic failure was 22% at 95% adherence or better, but 61% at 80-94.9% (Paterson et al. 2000). Later work showed that some regimens, notably non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors such as efavirenz, often suppress the virus at lower adherence (Bangsberg 2006).

Staying in care, collecting refills and taking pills every day are all harder for patients facing poverty, unstable housing, mental illness or substance use, which describes much of the population served by a county safety-net HIV clinic.

Pill burden and single-tablet regimens

One proposed help is to make the regimen simpler. A single-tablet regimen (STR) combines a complete regimen, usually three drugs, in one pill taken once a day. The first, efavirenz/tenofovir/emtricitabine (Atripla), was approved in the United States in July 2006 (FDA, 2006).

The case for STRs rests on adherence. A meta-analysis of randomized trials found that fewer pills a day went with both better adherence and better viral suppression (Nachega et al. 2014). In a large insurance database, patients on one pill a day were more likely to reach 95% adherence than those on three or more, and were hospitalized less (Sax et al. 2012). Whether that translates into better outcomes in a safety-net population, where the barriers are often about getting to the clinic and the pharmacy at all, was the question this study asked. The evidence on that is summarised under Other evidence.

The setting

Thomas Street Health Center is the main outpatient HIV clinic of Harris Health System, the publicly funded health system of Harris County, Texas, and the oldest free-standing HIV clinic in the United States (Flash et al. 2015). Most patients receive their medication from the clinic’s own pharmacy, which is what makes a study from pharmacy records possible: each fill is recorded, with the date and the drug, for every patient in the same way.

Measuring adherence from pharmacy records

Pharmacy fills record what a patient collected, not what they swallowed, but they are recorded for everyone without extra effort. Measures built from refill records have been used and checked against other measures of adherence for decades (Steiner and Prochazka 1997), and for ART, pharmacy-based measures consistently predict viral suppression, though which version predicts best is not settled (McMahon et al. 2011). This study used one of them, the proportion of days covered. How it is computed day by day, and what it misses (fills at other pharmacies, fills lasting longer than a month), is explained on Methodological background, shown in a figure on Study design and methods, and coded on 5. Pills on hand and daily outcomes.

The regimens compared

The study included patients starting ART from 2008 to 2011. At that time, first-line regimens at the clinic were built on tenofovir and emtricitabine:

The three starting regimens in the published comparison
Regimen Pills per day, 2008-2011 Group in this study
Efavirenz/tenofovir/emtricitabine (Atripla) 1 Single-tablet regimen (STR)
Darunavir + ritonavir + tenofovir/emtricitabine (Prezista + Norvir + Truvada) 4 (two 400 mg darunavir tablets) Multi-tablet regimen (MTR)
Atazanavir + ritonavir + tenofovir/emtricitabine (Reyataz + Norvir + Truvada) 3 Multi-tablet regimen (MTR)

So the comparison is one pill against three or four. A single 800 mg darunavir tablet was approved only in November 2012, after enrollment closed.

Rilpivirine/tenofovir/emtricitabine (Complera), a second STR, was approved in August 2011, at the very end of enrollment. Elvitegravir/cobicistat/tenofovir/emtricitabine (Stribild) came in August 2012, after it. Earlier versions of the analysis included other regimens, but the published comparison is limited to these three.

The comparison is not only about pill count. The STR contains a non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (efavirenz) and the MTRs a boosted protease inhibitor. Clinicians may also have chosen protease inhibitors for patients they expected to adhere poorly, because protease inhibitors are less prone to resistance. The paper discusses both limitations (Hemmige et al. 2018).

What the study found

All the numbers below are quoted from the paper (Hemmige et al. 2018). The code in this repository has not been run on the study data, so it has not reproduced them. Published results and the code behind them gives every number with the step of the code that computes it.

Patients who started the STR were more likely to be retained in care in their first year: 80.7% against 72.7%, with an odds ratio of 1.49 (95% CI 1.10-2.02) after propensity weighting.

They were also more likely to be suppressed at least once in the first year (84.4% against 77.6%; weighted odds ratio 1.41, 1.02-1.96). This counts a patient as suppressed if any viral load in the year met the definition. It does not mean they stayed suppressed.

Two results did not differ clearly between the groups after weighting:

  • Suppressed while still on the starting regimen: odds ratio 1.28 (0.95-1.73).
  • Adherence measured from pharmacy fills (at least 80% of days covered): odds ratio 1.04 (0.79-1.38).

These are associations from one clinic’s records, weighted for measured differences between the groups. They are not the result of a trial: patients the clinicians judged likely to struggle may have been steered to one regimen for reasons the data do not record.

How far the findings apply

  • One clinic. Thomas Street Health Center is a county safety-net clinic in Houston serving mainly low-income patients. Patients elsewhere, with different support, may differ.
  • Older regimens. Efavirenz-based regimens are no longer recommended first-line treatment in the United States, and today’s single tablets are mostly integrase-inhibitor based. The study speaks to the value of a simpler regimen, not to which drugs to start today.
  • Drug class as well as pill count. In a randomized trial, atazanavir/ritonavir and efavirenz had similar antiviral activity when each was given with tenofovir/emtricitabine (Daar et al. 2011). A difference in an observational study is therefore not simply the effect of the drugs themselves. It may come from fewer pills, from tolerability, or from who was given which regimen.
  • Starting regimen, not the regimen taken. Patients stay in the group of the regimen they started, even if they switched. Nine MTR patients switched to an STR during the year and are still counted as MTR, which would tend to shrink any difference between the groups.

Other evidence

Other studies of pill burden point in the same direction, but not uniformly:

  • A meta-analysis of randomized trials found that fewer pills a day went with both better adherence and better viral suppression (Nachega et al. 2014).
  • In commercially insured patients, one pill a day went with better adherence and fewer hospital stays than three or more (Sax et al. 2012).
  • Among homeless and marginally housed people in San Francisco, the efavirenz-based STR was associated with higher adherence and more suppression. The suppression difference disappeared once adherence was taken into account (Bangsberg et al. 2010).
  • In a cohort of women in the United States, STR use was associated with modestly better adherence and suppression (Hanna et al. 2014).
  • In South Carolina Medicaid, STRs were associated with better adherence and fewer hospital stays, but not with more suppression after adjustment (Sutton et al. 2016).
  • In a large French cohort, STRs lasted longer before being stopped, but outcomes were similar once switches made only to simplify treatment were not counted as failures (Cotte et al. 2017).
